Where should Delhi's public EV chargers go?

A comparative analysis of algorithms to optimise EV charging station locations in NCT Delhi. We ran three siting methods on the same estimate of where public charging for electric four-wheelers is needed: the government's grid rule, weighted K-means clustering and the Maximal Covering Location Problem. This site shows what each one produces, side by side, and explains why we don't rank them.

  • Weighted K-means · machine learning

The problem

More electric cars, and a city deciding where to plug them in

The Delhi Electric Vehicles Policy 2020 set a target of 25% battery electric vehicles among all new registrations (GNCTD Transport Department, n.d.). Charging is one of the main constraints: the Economic Survey of Delhi 2022–23 counted about 3,734 charging points at 2,610 locations, plus 234 battery-swapping stations, by the end of 2022, and the government's plan is to expand the network to 18,000 public charging points (GNCTD Planning Department, 2022).

14,016electric taxis registered at the citywide taxi unit (DL52)

Placement matters as much as the count. The Ministry of Power guideline asks for at least one charging station in every 3 km × 3 km grid section (Dash & Anup, 2024), a target the Delhi policy also adopts (GNCTD Transport Department, 2020), and the policy says two charging stations should be no more than 3 km apart (Vansola et al., 2023). That spacing implies a worst-case trip of roughly 2.1 km to the nearest station, which is the coverage radius used throughout this site.

Several methods have been proposed for choosing sites, but they are usually tested one at a time, on different cities and different data. This project runs three of them on one Delhi dataset so their outputs can be put next to each other.

Study area and demand

One demand estimate, shared by all three methods

The study area is Delhi's 250 Municipal Corporation (MCD) wards from the 2022 delimitation, 1,393 km² in all. The New Delhi Municipal Council (NDMC) area and Delhi Cantonment are not MCD wards, so they are outside the study area. Ward populations are Census 2011 counts, 16,419,787 people in total.

We cut the study area into 1,570 cells of 1 km × 1 km (cells on the boundary are clipped) and gave each cell a weight for how much public charging it is likely to need. The weights sum to 1,000, so a cell's weight is its share of the total in tenths of a percent.

Where people live: population, spread over mapped residential land within each ward60%

w_c = 1000 · s_c / Σs, where s_c = 0.6 · R_c/ΣR + 0.4 · D_c/ΣD

R is the home side and D the destination side; within points of interest, offices and malls get 25% each, hospitals 20%, hotels and parking 15% each. These shares are the team's own reasoned starting values, not taken from the literature. Where a ward has almost no mapped residential land (33 of 250 wards), its population is spread by area instead.

The three methods

Three methods, three different goals

All three methods place stations on the same map, but each one is built to pursue a different goal. That difference matters for reading the results.

Policy rule

Grid

Lay a square grid over Delhi. Every cell where at least 1% of the land is commercial or industrial gets one station, at the cell's centre (or the centre of its largest part inside the study area). This is the Ministry of Power 3 km rule, applied with the ICCT method (Dash & Anup, 2024; Vansola et al., 2023).

What it pursues

Compliance with a spacing rule. It doesn't read the demand weights, and the number of stations follows from the rule rather than being chosen.

Rule

station in cell g if C/I land share ≥ 1%

Cell size
1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5 km (3 km baseline)
Stations
404 at 1 km to 48 at 5 km; 104 at 3 km
Machine learning

Weighted K-means

Group the 1,570 demand points into k clusters and put a station at each cluster's demand-weighted centre. Heavily weighted cells pull stations towards them (after Abdullahi et al., 2024).

What it pursues

Short distances on average: it minimises the demand-weighted squared distance from each cell to its nearest station.

Objective

min Σ w_i · min_j ‖x_i − c_j‖²

Stations
k from 48 to 404 (28 values)
Setup
k-means++ start, 10 restarts, fixed random seed
Optimisation

MCLP

The Maximal Covering Location Problem (Church & ReVelle, 1974; Frade et al., 2011) chooses p sites from the 1,570 cell centres to maximise the demand within R = 2.1 km of a station. Once all demand is covered, a second stage places the remaining stations to maximise demand within 1 km.

What it pursues

Coverage: as much demand as possible within the radius, for a fixed number of stations.

Formulation

max Σ w_i y_i subject to y_i ≤ Σ_{j in N_i} x_j for all i; Σ x_j = p; x_j ∈ {0,1}; 0 ≤ y_i ≤ 1

Radius
2.1 km straight-line
Stations
p from 48 to 404 (28 values)
Solver
HiGHS (via SciPy), 90 s limit per stage

Why there's no winner

We don't rank the methods, because every yardstick belongs to one of them

We set out to compare the three methods and recommend one. Once the results were in, we concluded that an unbiased ranking isn't possible. Each method optimises a different objective, and every figure we could use to judge them is one of those objectives:

Gridis built to satisfy a spacing rule on commercial and industrial land. Judged on compliance with that rule, it meets it by construction.
Weighted K-meansis built to keep stations close to demand on average. Judged on mean distance, it is scored on something very close to its own objective, which uses squared distance.
MCLPis built to cover as much demand as possible within the radius. Judged on demand within 2.1 km, it is aiming at that number directly.

So choosing the column to sort by is the same as choosing the method. Combining several columns into one score doesn't avoid this: the result then depends on the weights given to each column, which is the same choice made less visibly. The figures also move with our own assumptions, such as the 60/40 split in the demand estimate and the coverage radius: MCLP is solved for 2.1 km, so at 1.5 km or 3 km its layouts are measured against a radius they weren't solved for.

What the site does instead is show each method's output on the same demand estimate, report the same figures for all three, and let you change the settings. Which trade-off suits Delhi is a planning decision, and this is meant to inform it rather than make it.

The consensus sites in the explorer are one way to read the results without ranking them: places where both K-means and MCLP have a station within 1 km of a grid station, for the settings shown.

Data and limitations

Where the data comes from, and what it can't tell us

Sources

  • Ward boundaries and population: the 2022 MCD ward delimitation (250 wards). Its population figures are Census 2011 counts. Source file: [TBD: source URL for the 2022 ward boundary file]
  • Land use, points of interest, roads and transit: OpenStreetMap. We used OSM land use because the DDA Master Plan maps are published only as PDFs. Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
  • Existing chargers: 1,404 points from SwitchDelhi (1,322) and OpenStreetMap (82), with duplicates within 30 m merged; 1,323 of them are inside the study area. A charger counts as a four-wheeler site if it matches, within 30 m, a SwitchDelhi listing whose vehicle type includes 4W. That leaves 98 four-wheeler chargers in the study area. OpenStreetMap points carry no vehicle type, so none of them count as four-wheeler sites.
  • EV registrations: VAHAN (Ministry of Road Transport & Highways), by RTO: active vehicles registered from 2020 to 2026, with 2026 a part year.

Limitations

  • OSM land-use coverage in Delhi is patchy. Missing residential, commercial or industrial polygons shift both the demand estimate and the grid rule, which depends on commercial and industrial land.
  • Charger data may undercount. The 98 four-wheeler chargers are those we could identify; OpenStreetMap points have no vehicle type.
  • VAHAN registrations are available only by RTO, not by ward, and the demand estimate doesn't use them directly. Electric cars in VAHAN also include hybrids, some of which never plug in.
  • Population is from Census 2011. Delhi has grown since, unevenly across wards.
  • The destination weights are our own starting values, not taken from the literature.
  • Distances are straight-line. On 118 pairs of wards 1.5–5 km apart, the road distance was a median 1.42 times the straight-line distance, so 2.1 km straight-line is roughly 3 km by road.
  • There is no utilisation data, so the demand estimate is a proxy for where charging is needed, not a measure of charging sessions.
  • MCLP layouts for 70–140 stations are near-optimal, not proven optimal: the solver stopped at its 90-second limit. The other station counts were solved to proven optimality.
  • Land availability and grid capacity aren't modelled. A site on the map is a location, not a confirmed plot with a power connection.
