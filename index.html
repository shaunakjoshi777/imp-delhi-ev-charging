Why public chargers

Delhi’s electric cars need somewhere to plug in

Delhi has about 82,000 electric four-wheelers (cars and taxis) and 98 public charging sites confirmed to serve them inside our study area. The question: if the city builds a fixed number of new public charging stations, where could they go?

In India, home charging delivers about 80–90% of daily EV charging energy. But only about 55% of people who want an EV can charge at home, and 70–75% of urban households live in apartments (Kazam & AEEE, 2026). Public stations serve people who cannot charge at home and drivers who top up while out.

The Delhi Electric Vehicles Policy 2020 set a target of 25% battery electric vehicles among new registrations (GNCTD Transport Department, 2020). The Ministry of Power guideline asks for at least one public charging station in every 3 km × 3 km grid square in cities (Ministry of Power, 2022). Delhi’s action plan aims for a public charger within 3 km of travel from anywhere (Delhi EV Cell).

Study area and demand

One demand map, built from where people live and where they go

Nobody has measured charging demand, so we estimate it for 1,570 squares of 1 km × 1 km. All weights add up to 1,000, so a square with weight 4 holds 0.4% of estimated demand. The grid method does not read this map.

  1. Home demand (60%). Each ward’s population is placed on land mapped as residential, then multiplied by the ward’s EV registrations per resident. In 33 wards with under 1% residential land, population is spread by area.
  2. Destination demand (40%). Each square gets 0–1 rank scores from OpenStreetMap features, combined with the weights below and multiplied by area.
  3. Blend. Each part becomes a share of Delhi’s total, then they are mixed 60:40.

The three methods

How each method places stations

Grid (policy rule)

Answers: which areas qualify under the rule?

  1. Cover the study area with 3 km squares (206 touch Delhi).
  2. Measure the share of land mapped as commercial, retail or industrial.
  3. Give a station to every square with at least 1%: 104 stations.
  4. Place it at the square’s centre, or the centre of its largest part inside Delhi.

Assumption: a little commercial or industrial land signals enough activity for a station. Each qualifying square gets exactly one.

Source: Dash & Anup (2024); Vansola et al. (2023).

Weighted K-means (machine learning)

Answers: where are the centres of demand?

  1. Start with k centres spread out by k-means++.
  2. Assign every demand square to its nearest centre.
  3. Move each centre to the demand-weighted average of its squares.
  4. Repeat until nothing moves; run 20 times and keep the lowest-error result.

It shortens the demand-weighted squared distance to a station. A centre falling outside the study area would be moved to the nearest point inside; none needed it. In repeated runs, 92 of 104 stations reappear within 2 km in at least 80% of runs.

Source: after Abdullahi et al. (2024).

MCLP (optimisation)

Answers: which sites reach the most demand within a set distance?

It picks exactly p sites from a candidate list so that the most demand is within 2.1 km. Candidates: 206 points on the 3 km grid (team baseline) or 1,570 square centres. Ties are broken by demand within 1 km. Solved with HiGHS through SciPy; the largest runs stop at a time limit. In repeated runs, 89 of 104 stations stay at the same site in at least 80% of runs.

Source: Church & ReVelle (1974); Frade et al. (2011).

Scenarios and station numbers

What you can change, and why 104 is the starting point

  • Scenario A, new network: stations are placed as if the city had none.
  • Scenario B, build on existing charger locations: the 98 confirmed four-wheeler sites stay open. The grid skips squares that already have one; K-means holds them fixed; MCLP keeps them and chooses new sites.
  • Why 104: it is the number the grid rule produces at 3 km. It is a shared starting point, not a cost or need estimate. Elbow and silhouette tests gave no clear number.
  • Policy context: guaranteeing a charger within 3 km by road from every square needs about 224 stations (set-covering model, Toregas et al., 1971).
  • Coverage distance 2.1 km: the farthest any point in a 3 km square is from its centre.

How to read the figures: coverage is the share within the chosen distance of a station. Mean distance is weighted by demand. The 95th percentile is the distance 95% of demand falls within. The farthest square is the one farthest from any station.

Method

A shared site is a grid station that MCLP (206 sites on the 3 km grid) also chooses and that has a K-means station within 1 km. Because methods built on different logic chose these places, the team treats them as the safest first-phase locations. Grid and MCLP pick from the same 3 km points, so part of their agreement is built in; K-means is the independent check.

Limitations

  • No charging-session data exists, so demand is an estimate from proxies.
  • EV registrations are known only by RTO zone. Wards around Burari are likely over-weighted. The counts include hybrids that never plug in.
  • Population is from Census 2011.
  • OpenStreetMap is incomplete; commercial land and parking are undercounted.
  • Road distances are about 1.4–1.6 times straight-line distances.
  • Stations are assumed to have unlimited capacity.
  • Grid capacity and land availability are not modelled. Limiting sites to mapped parking and fuel stations lowers coverage by 6–9 points.
  • Income does not vary by ward in the data, so it cannot be used to check fairness.
  • NDMC and Delhi Cantonment are excluded, so border stations are slightly under-weighted.
  • Four-wheelers only.
